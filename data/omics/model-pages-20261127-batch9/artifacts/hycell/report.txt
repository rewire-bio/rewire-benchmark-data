HyCell: a Compact Embedding Model
for Single-Cell Transcriptomes
Hyra Team

Abstract
Pretrained single-cell models are used as frozen encoders that map the transcriptome
of a cell to an embedding for annotation, integration and comparison across studies,
and recent models have grown to hundreds of millions of parameters. We present
HyCell, a single-cell embedding model with 16 million parameters trained on 79.5
million human cells from CELLxGENE Census. HyCell encodes the expressed genes
of a cell as an unordered set with a six-layer transformer and reconstructs its counts
through a negative-binomial decoder that also receives learned dataset and donor codes,
while a donor discriminator on the embedding removes donor signal that remains;
batch and donor information is used only in training. We compare frozen, zero-shot
embeddings of HyCell with those of ten published single-cell foundation models, among
them Stack, SCimilarity, scFoundation, UCE, scGPT, Geneformer and STATE, on seven
atlases excluded from pretraining, with every model rerun on the same cells and scored
by the same code. HyCell has the highest scIB biological-conservation score of the eleven
models (0.700, against 0.699 for SCimilarity, which is trained with cell-type labels), and
its overall score (0.735), which combines biological conservation, batch correction and
annotation transfer, is level with that of Stack in its recommended batch-grouped mode
(0.736), which uses batch labels at inference and has 14 times more parameters, and
above those of the other nine models. In downstream analyses, HyCell embeddings
separate naive from effector CD4 T cells across donors and sequencing chemistries (ARI
0.64, against at most 0.03 for every other embedding) and order B cells into plasma cells
more faithfully than any other embedding, and the decoder reads out the corresponding
gene programs consistently across donors.

1

Introduction

Single-cell foundation models are pretrained on tens to hundreds of millions of transcriptomes and
then used as frozen encoders, whose embeddings of new cells serve to annotate cell types, integrate
datasets and compare cell states across studies (Cui et al., 2024; Theodoris et al., 2023; Rosen et al.,
2026; Hao et al., 2024). Recent models have grown to hundreds of millions or billions of parameters
(Adduri et al., 2026; Gandhi et al., 2025; Pearce et al., 2026), yet independent evaluations find that their
zero-shot embeddings often separate cell types no better than principal component analysis (PCA) of
the counts (Kedzierska et al., 2025). A central difficulty is technical variation. Cells from different
donors, laboratories and sequencing chemistries differ systematically. A reconstruction loss is lowered by
encoding these differences, and the embedding then mixes cell identity with batch.
HyCell addresses this in the training objective rather than in model size. Its encoder maps the expressed
genes of a cell to a 512-dimensional embedding z, and a count decoder reconstructs the full expression
profile from z. The decoder also receives a learned code for the dataset and the donor of each cell, so
dataset- and donor-specific variation can be explained without passing through z. A donor discriminator
trained on z adds pressure to remove what remains. Its transformer has 4.8 million parameters, and the
whole model, including a gene embedding table shared by encoder and decoder, has 16 million, fewer
than any model we compare with and 42 times fewer than UCE.
We evaluate HyCell against ten published single-cell foundation models on seven atlases that were
excluded from pretraining, rerunning every model with its released weights on the same cells and scoring
all embeddings with the same code. Our main results are the following.
• Highest biological conservation. HyCell has the highest scIB biological-conservation score of the
eleven models, 0.700 averaged over the seven atlases, level with SCimilarity (0.699), which is trained
with cell-type labels, and at least 0.038 above every other self-supervised model (Figure 1).

2

Model

HyCell is an encoder–decoder model trained to reconstruct the expression profile of a cell (Figure 2). The
encoder turns the expressed genes of a cell into an embedding z ∈ R512 , which is the representation used
in all evaluations. The decoder and the donor discriminator are used only during training.
2.1

Cells as gene sets

The gene vocabulary has 36,495 genes, the 20,080 protein-coding genes together with every gene detected
in at least 0.1% of corpus cells, and covers 99.9% of the nonzero entries of the corpus. A cell is represented
as an unordered set of tokens with no positional encoding. Each expressed gene contributes a token
equal to the gene’s embedding plus a two-layer network applied to its log(1 + x )-transformed expression
per 10,000 counts. Up to 818 expressed genes enter the set; for cells that express more, genes are sampled
without replacement with probability increasing in their expression. To each cell we add measured zeros,
genes that the dataset measured but the cell did not express, in number equal to a quarter of the expressed
genes. Zeros are drawn only from genes measured in at least 90% of training datasets, so that the gene
panel of a dataset does not identify it. With a [CLS] token, a set has at most 1,024 tokens.
During training, two augmentations make the input noisier than the target. With probability 0.3 a
random 15–100% subset of the genes is kept, and with probability 0.5 the counts are binomially thinned
to 10–100% of their depth. The decoder always reconstructs the full, unaugmented profile. Embeddings
for evaluation are computed without augmentation and with a fixed per-cell sampling seed.
2.2

Encoder

The encoder is a pre-normalisation transformer with RMSNorm, six layers of width 256, four attention
heads and SwiGLU feed-forward blocks of width 704. Attention is bidirectional over sets of variable size
packed without padding. The embedding z is a linear projection of the final [CLS] state to 512 dimensions.

transformer encoder of six attention and SwiGLU blocks maps the set to the embedding z. During training, a count

The transformer has 4.8 million parameters; with the gene embedding table, which the decoder shares,
and the decoder itself, HyCell has 16.1 million (Table 1).
2.3

Count decoder with dataset and donor codes

The decoder maps z through a two-layer network (512 → 1,024 → 256, SiLU) to a vector h, and scores
each gene g by ℓ g = h · t g + bg , where t g is the gene’s input embedding and bg a gene bias. The mean
count of gene g is the library size times the softmax of ℓ over the genes measured in the cell’s dataset, and
counts follow a negative-binomial distribution with a learned dispersion per gene (Lopez et al., 2018).
Genes the dataset did not measure do not enter the likelihood, so missing genes are never treated as
zeros.
The decoder is conditioned on a 64-dimensional code e = Edataset + Edonor , the sum of a learned embedding of the cell’s dataset (555 entries) and of its donor within that dataset (12,533 entries). The code
enters both decoder layers. Differences in depth, chemistry, ambient RNA and donor background that
are shared by the cells of a dataset or a donor can be absorbed by e, and reconstructing them does not
require z to encode them. A second loss term, with weight 0.1, reconstructs each cell from a stop-gradient
copy of the decoder input with the code replaced by a learned unknown-donor or unknown-dataset code,
so the decoder also works for cells from datasets outside the corpus.
2.4

Donor adversary

A discriminator, a two-layer network (512 → 256 → 256, LeakyReLU with slope 0.2, dropout 0.3), predicts
the donor of each cell among the donors of its dataset from z. It is trained on detached embeddings with
a cross-entropy loss. The encoder is trained, through the discriminator with its weights held fixed, to
make the predicted donor distribution match the donor composition of the cell’s dataset (Ganin et al.,
2016). The weight of this term starts after 2 × 108 tokens and rises smoothly to 0.01. Only datasets with at
least two donors contribute.
The training loss is the negative-binomial reconstruction loss, plus 0.1 times the reconstruction loss under
the unknown code, plus the weighted donor-confusion loss.
2.5

Pretraining data and schedule

The corpus is the human primary-cell data with raw counts in the CZ CELLxGENE Census release of 8
November 2025 (CZI Cell Science Program et al., 2025), 95.0 million cells from 555 datasets. We remove
every dataset and collection that contributes cells to an evaluation atlas (Section 3.1) or to a separate
development set of eight tissues, which we used for all design choices, leaving 79.5 million training cells
from 532 datasets and 20,000 validation cells. Datasets are sampled with probability proportional to the
square root of their size, and cells uniformly within a dataset.

3

Benchmark

3.1

Protocol

Atlases. We evaluate on seven atlases with raw integer counts and curated cell-type labels (Table 2).
Three are the integer-count parts of the scIB integration benchmarks for human immune cells, pancreas
and lung (Luecken et al., 2022). The others are peripheral blood mononuclear cells from two 10x Genomics
runs (10x Genomics, 2017), the healthy donors of the MultiVINE atlas of the human brain vasculature,
released in 2025 (Reid et al., 2025), a stratified 40,322-cell sample of the cross-tissue immune atlas of
Domínguez Conde et al. (2022), and a 41,140-cell sample of the Asian Immune Diversity Atlas (Kock et al.,
2025). Every dataset and collection that contributes cells to these atlases is excluded from the pretraining
corpus.
Metrics. All embeddings are scored by one implementation, built on scib-metrics (Gayoso et al.,
2026), on the absolute scale without rescaling across methods. Biological conservation (Bio) is the
mean of seven scIB metrics: isolated-label score, NMI and ARI of k-means and of Leiden clusters
against the cell types, cell-type silhouette and cLISI. Batch correction (Batch) is the mean of five: batch
silhouette, iLISI, kBET, graph connectivity and principal-component regression. Annotation transfer holds
out each batch in turn, trains a logistic-regression classifier (LR) and a 15-nearest-neighbour classifier
(kNN) on the remaining batches, and reports macro-F1 averaged over held-out batches. The score
S = 0.3 Bio + 0.2 Batch + 0.25 LR + 0.25 kNN weights the scIB total (0.6 Bio + 0.4 Batch) and annotation
transfer equally. Atlases are weighted equally in all averages.
Models. We compare with Stack (Dong et al., 2026), SCimilarity (Heimberg et al., 2025), scFoundation
(Hao et al., 2024), UCE (Rosen et al., 2026), Tahoe-x1 (Gandhi et al., 2025), TranscriptFormer (Pearce
et al., 2026), scGPT (Cui et al., 2024), Geneformer V2 (Chen et al., 2026), STATE (Adduri et al., 2026) and
scPRINT (Kalfon et al., 2025), each run by us with its released weights and default inference settings
on the same cells. Stack encodes sets of 256 cells, and its released tutorial orders cells by donor before
encoding; we encode the cells of each batch together, so Stack uses batch labels at inference. SCimilarity
and scPRINT are trained with cell-type labels. As a reference without pretraining we fit PCA to each
atlas (2,000 highly variable genes of the log-normalised counts, 50 components). HyCell embeds each cell
from its counts alone, without batch or donor information.

Results

Table 3 summarises the seven atlases and Table 4 gives the score of every model on each.
Cell identity. HyCell has the highest biological-conservation score of all models, 0.700. SCimilarity,
trained with cell-type labels, follows at 0.699, and the best self-supervised model, UCE, at 0.662. Among
self-supervised models, HyCell ranks first on five of the seven atlases and shares first place on the brain
atlas (Figure 1b).
Overall score. HyCell reaches S = 0.735, level with Stack (0.736) and ahead of SCimilarity (0.726),
scFoundation (0.702) and UCE (0.694). Stack scores higher on batch correction (0.596 against 0.527), with
sets of cells grouped by batch at inference; HyCell scores higher on biological conservation (0.700 against
0.657) with 14 times fewer parameters. Among models that embed each cell on its own, HyCell has the
best batch-correction score after SCimilarity. In annotation transfer HyCell is second on both classifiers,
by 0.002 behind scFoundation for LR and by 0.006 behind batch-grouped Stack for kNN, and ahead of all
other models on both. These comparisons concern cell-level tasks. Stack also draws on the other cells of a
sample at inference, which helps tasks defined per sample, such as predicting a donor’s disease state or
the perturbation applied to a well; such tasks are not part of this benchmark.
Size. The models within 0.03 of HyCell in S have 2 to 14 times more parameters, and the three largest
models, UCE, STATE and TranscriptFormer with 429 to 675 million parameters, score between 0.659 and
0.694 (Figure 1a).

Downstream biological analysis

We apply the frozen embeddings to the cross-tissue immune atlas, where cells of the same state come
from 12 donors, 17 tissue sites and three 10x chemistries. We separate naive from effector CD4 T cells,
order B cells along their differentiation into plasma cells (Figure 4), and read out the gene programs of
these states with the decoder (Figure 5).

naive and 96% effector cells (ARI 0.64), and the split is nearly independent of sequencing chemistry (NMI
with chemistry 0.012). With every other embedding, Stack and PCA included, the two groups do not

4.2

From B cells to plasma cells

Naive B cells differentiate through germinal-centre B cells into antibody-secreting plasmablasts and
plasma cells. For the 3,751 cells of these four states (1,440, 572, 618 and 1,121 cells), we computed
diffusion pseudotime (Haghverdi et al., 2016) with Scanpy (Wolf et al., 2018) on each embedding, rooted
at the medoid of the 50 cells with the highest naive-B marker score (TCL1A, IGHD, FCER2). In HyCell
the median pseudotime rises from 0.04 for naive B cells through 0.55 and 0.84 to 0.92 for plasma cells
(Figure 4b), and the Spearman correlation between pseudotime and stage, 0.937, is the highest of all
embeddings. Averaged over donors it is 0.894, again the highest, ahead of Stack (0.918 and 0.879),
SCimilarity (0.908 and 0.868) and PCA (0.888 and 0.849) (Figure 4c). Eight of the ten published models
order the stages less faithfully than PCA of the counts.
4.3

Gene programs from the decoder

Because HyCell is trained to reconstruct counts, its decoder turns an embedding back into expected
expression. Decoding the embedding of each cell with the unknown-dataset code and averaging within a
state gives a gene-expression profile per state computed from the embeddings, and the ratio between two
states gives a decoded fold change for every gene.
From naive B cells to plasma cells, the decoded fold changes recover the antibody-secretion program
(Figure 5a). The genes decoded as most increased in plasma cells are JCHAIN, SSR4, MZB1, XBP1, PRDX4
and HSP90B1, together with the endoplasmic-reticulum machinery DERL3, FKBP11, SEC11C and HSPA5;
the most decreased are the HLA class II genes HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DPB1, which
plasma cells lose with antigen presentation, and the lymphocyte genes CD37, CD52, CXCR4 and LTB.
Over the 12,879 genes detected in at least 1% of either state, decoded and counted fold changes have a
Spearman correlation of 0.81 (Figure 5b).
The decoded programs are stable across donors. We compared the program of each donor with that of
all other donors. For the plasma-cell axis the mean Spearman correlation is 0.965 for the decoder and
0.772 for pseudobulk counts; for classical against nonclassical monocytes it is 0.940 against 0.476, and for
naive against effector CD4 T cells 0.957 against 0.376 (Figure 5c). The decoder thus provides a gene-level

Fine-tuning for cell-type annotation

With a labelled reference, HyCell can be fine-tuned into a cell-type annotator. The encoder and a linear
classification head on the embedding are trained together with cross-entropy, starting from the pretrained
weights. Each step draws 64 labelled cells and tokenizes them with the pretraining augmentations
(Section 2.1); the network is trained with a learning rate of 10−5 and the head with 10−3 , for at most 2,000
steps, with early stopping on 10% of the reference cells.
We evaluated this procedure by leave-one-batch-out annotation on four of the benchmark atlases. For
every batch, the annotator is fine-tuned on the other batches and predicts the held-out batch. The
baselines are the logistic-regression and kNN classifiers of the benchmark, trained on frozen HyCell
embeddings of the same reference cells. On all four atlases the fine-tuned annotator has the highest mean
macro-F1 (Table 5), and it is ahead of logistic regression on 19 of the 20 held-out batches and of kNN
on 17. The largest gain, from 0.838 to 0.907, is on the pancreas atlas, whose batches come from different
sequencing technologies. Fine-tuning takes one to three minutes on one NVIDIA H20 for 10,000 to 31,000
reference cells.

Availability

HyCell is distributed with the hycell Python package, which embeds cells from the raw counts of
AnnData objects or .h5ad files, decodes embeddings into gene expression, and fine-tunes cell-type
annotators, from Python and from the command line. On a GPU with flash-attention the package
reproduces the embeddings scored in this report bit for bit. HyCell embeds every cell on its own and is
trained to discount donor and dataset effects, which suits comparisons of cells across donors and studies;
properties shared by all cells of a donor, such as sex or disease state, are better read from embeddings
aggregated per donor or sample.

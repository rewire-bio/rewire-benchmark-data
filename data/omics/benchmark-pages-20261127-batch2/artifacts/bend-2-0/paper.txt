                                                                                            A BSTRACT

                                                      Genomic foundation models are increasingly used to propose DNA sequences and
                                                      edits. But successful design requires capabilities that current benchmarks do not
                                                      measure. Here we introduce BEND 2.0 (BENchmark for DNA language models),
                                                      which tests how well models predict the effects of DNA changes and whether these
                                                      predictions can guide sequence design. Across eight tasks built on five experimental
                                                      datasets, we find that model choice and how representations are accessed both
                                                      affect performance. Linear readouts fitted to frozen representations can match or
                                                      exceed models’ native outputs, and functional post-training substantially improves
                                                      the models’ capabilities. Linear readouts transfer well across experimental assays
                                                      and genes, suggesting that models capture functional patterns beyond the data used
                                                      to fit individual predictors. At the same time, strong prediction of average effects
                                                      can conceal weaker prediction of context-specific effects, and accurate prediction of
                                                      combined mutation effects does not imply accurate prediction of their interactions.
                                                      In design tasks, model predictions can reduce the number of candidates needed to
                                                      find a desired response, but still leave substantial room for better design choices,
                                                      particularly for context-specific and multi-cell objectives. Our benchmark reveals
                                                      that genomic models now display signs of general utility but have yet to saturate
                                                      on performance in even simple tasks.

                                           1     I NTRODUCTION

                                           A biologist seeking to change the activity of a regulatory sequence may have hundreds of mutations
                                           to choose from. The same mutation can have different effects in different cell types or even on a
                                           slightly different genetic background. To help choose what to test, models must distinguish small
                                           changes most likely to produce a fine-grained desired response.
                                           Genomic models use different approaches to learn the complex and multifaceted patterns encoded in
                                           DNA, drawing on both sequence diversity and measurements of biological function. Models such as
                                           Evo2 and DNABERT-2 learn from natural sequence variation (Brixi et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2024),
                                           while GPN-Star uses evolutionary relationships to predict functional constraint (Ye et al., 2026).
                                           Other models incorporate functional outcomes rather than relying on natural sequence variation
                                           and homology. AlphaGenome, for example, predicts expression, accessibility and splicing from
                                           functional genomic data (Avsec et al., 2026), while NTv3 combines sequence learning with such
                                           functional supervision (Boshar et al., 2025). Foundation models and task-specific models have guided
                                           the design of sequences with cell type-targeted activity (Gosai et al., 2024) and, more recently, viable
                                           bacteriophage genomes (King et al., 2026). However, the central question remains: which features of
                                           sequence and function have these models learned well enough to guide experiments?

Looking only at overall predictive accuracy leaves that question unanswered. A sequence-based score
may identify a mutation at an evolutionarily constrained position without predicting its effect on
expression in a particular cell type. Accurate predictions for individual mutations do not establish
whether a model captures interactions between mutations or can predict their effects on a different
genetic background. Similarly, distinguishing highly active genes from weakly active genes does not
indicate which edit will produce a desired change in expression. Each of these cases calls for more
precise testing.
Building on BEND (Marin et al., 2024), we introduce BEND 2.0 to test whether genomic models
can guide DNA design. The benchmark is organized around five key capabilities: Prior, Function,
Context, Interaction, and Design. Together, our tasks measure what genomic models can predict and
whether they can help select DNA sequences that produce a desired response. Table 1 summarizes
our selected evaluations.

Related works. The original BEND benchmark (Marin et al., 2024), GenBench (Liu et al., 2024),
and OmniGenBench (Yang et al., 2025) provide systematic evaluations of genomic representations
across downstream tasks. In parallel, model-guided regulatory sequence design has demonstrated
cell type-targeted activity experimentally (Gosai et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2025; Castillo-Hair et al.,
2025), and NucleoBench (Shor et al., 2025) evaluates algorithms for searching sequence space using
predictive models as scoring functions. BEND 2.0 connects these directions by testing the predictive
distinctions that highly matter for design: resolving context-specific effects, predicting interactions
between mutations, and distinguishing candidates with different functional outcomes.

2    A FRAMEWORK FOR EVALUATING GENOMIC MODELS
BEND 2.0 is centered around five complementary capabilities that connect sequence and functional
understanding to genomic design.

Sequence priors: How probable is the DNA? We measure how well a model predicts a masked
base xi from surrounding DNA:
                              pθ (xi | available DNA context)                        (1)

This is computed via the negative log-probability, − log2 pθ (xi | available DNA context), over all
queried positions. We report bits per base where lower values indicate better predictions.

Functional prediction: Can sequence predict a functional outcome? For a reference sequence
x and an edit δ, the mutation effect ∆y is the change in an experimental measurement between x and
x + δ. A predictor fθ estimates this effect:
                                                      fθ
                                          (x, x + δ) −−→ ∆y
                                                         c                                           (2)
We test mutation-effect predictions on held-out genes, then evaluate whether the fitted outputs transfer
to another assay.

Biological context: How does genomic function depend on the context? For a DNA sequence
with an experimental measurement yc measured in several cells or tissues c, we separate its mean
across the evaluated contexts ȳ from the context-specific deviation κc :
                              yc =          ȳ              +             κc                         (3)
                                           |{z}                          |{z}
                                     mean across contexts       context-specific deviation

We test mutation-effect differences between cells, expression differences between tissues, and whether
predicted profiles across held-out genes identify the correct tissue.

Genetic interactions: Does one mutation alter another’s effect? Let ∆y1 and ∆y2 denote the
effects of two individual mutations, and ∆y1+2 their combined effect, all relative to a reference
sequence in the same context. The interaction I1,2 is the non-additive part of the signal:
                                 ∆y1+2 = ∆y1 + ∆y2 + I1,2                                            (4)
                                         | {z }      |{z}
                                               additive expectation       interaction

We hence score interaction separately since an accurate combined effect can mask poor predictions
of interaction.

Genomic design: Which candidate achieves the desired response? Given a target response
ytarget , the model chooses a candidate a⋆ among permitted edits or sequences C whose predicted
response y b (a) best matches the target ytarget :
                                     a⋆ = arg min ℓ(b
                                                    y(a), ytarget )                                  (5)
                                               a∈C

The loss ℓ measures mismatch with the target, using the responses relevant to each task: mutation
effects or activity across cells.

Predictions and generalization. Most predictions use linear heads fitted to frozen model sequence
embeddings. For mutation effects, the linear head weights w are fitted to the difference between
variant and reference embeddings (ϕθ (x)) with ∆y c = w⊤ [ϕθ (x + δ) − ϕθ (x)]. These predictions
and native outputs are reported separately. Detailed descriptions of the output types can be found
in Appendix A.1. We compute correlations within each gene or regulatory sequence, then average

across the G groups using ρmacro = G−1 g=1 ρg . This avoids the score being dominated by, e.g.,
one large gene. For evaluation splits, we group related examples by gene, regulatory element, locus
or sequence family. In addition to genomic foundation models, our comparisons include simple
predictors, specialist models and experimental references where applicable.


3    S EQUENCE PRIORS : MEASURING WHAT MODELS KNOW ABOUT DNA

The models learn useful sequence priors. To compare causal and masked sequence models, we
mask out the central base of 512 GRCh38 windows of 1,200bp. For this base reconstruction, NTv3
650M post and Evo2 have the lowest losses among models in Figure 1A. The foundation models
outperform simple base composition statistics, but do not seem to have converged on prediction
accuracy. For all NTv3 models, we used a fixed 12% additional masking from its training objective,
and the final scores averaged the central-base loss over two draws.

Learned sequence knowledge is not always readily accessible. For NTv3 650M post, hiding
only the central base gives a loss of 2.199 bits. Masking an additional 12% reduces this to 1.375, the
lowest among tested models, although heavier masking eventually worsens performance (Figure 1B).
Averaging predicted probabilities across eight masking draws reduces loss further, to 1.346. We tested
whether a fixed shift in internal activations could reproduce the benefit of additional masking. We
estimated activation differences between single-mask and extra-masked queries, selected layer and
shift strength on separate data, and evaluated the shifted model on single-mask queries from held-out
genomic blocks. For NTv3 650M post, accuracy rose from 29% to 56%, close to the pre-trained
model’s 55% without changing weights. Full results, controls and uncertainty estimates are provided
in Appendix B.1.
A simpler adjustment substantially improves GENERATORv2: temperature calibration lowers loss
from 2.168 to 1.711 while leaving accuracy at 50.2% (Figure 1C). Together, these results show that
measured sequence-prior performance depends on how the model is queried and its outputs are used.
These gains do not establish that sequence priors capture functional effects or variation between
individuals. Evo2 and CENO reconstruct 55.2/62.4% of assay reference bases, yet their allele scores
correlate only 0.045/0.029 with reporter effects (Appendix B.4). Our main prior evaluation uses
GRCh38; extending strong sequence priors to natural haplotypes remains an important next step
(evident by results in Appendix B.3).

4    F UNCTIONAL PREDICTION : FROM SEQUENCE TO EXPERIMENTAL RESPONSE

Mutation-effect prediction transfers. Next, we explore whether genomic models can predict how
mutations affect RNA splicing. OpenSplice measures changes in exon inclusion across 590,104
mutations in 466 genes (Quarantani et al., 2026). On held-out genes, AlphaGenome’s embedding-
fitted linear head reaches macro-gene Spearman 0.650, compared with 0.621 for its native outputs
and 0.564 for NTv3 650M post (Figure 2A). An older specialist model SpliceAI (Jaganathan et al.,
2019) reaches 0.674, higher than the top-performing foundation models.
NTv3 enables a comparison of the same architecture before and after functional training (post-
training). Post-training improves mutation-effect prediction at both model sizes, and the readout from
the 650M post-trained model significantly outperforms both a readout from the matched architecture

Editing efficiency depends on genomic context. The K562 TRIP library tests editing efficiency
across genomic locations while holding the reporter and editing program fixed (Mathis et al., 2025).
Both post-trained NTv3 models improve over their pre-trained counterparts, while their differences
from AlphaGenome are not statistically resolved (Appendix C.1).


5   B IOLOGICAL CONTEXT : HOW DOES FUNCTION DEPEND ON THE CONTEXT

Mutation effects differ between cells. Models may easily predict a mutation’s average effect yet
struggle to predict such effect when conditioned on the biological context. Commonly, one may be
interested in specific tissues or cell types. In the Siraj dataset, the same mutations are measured in
both HepG2 and K562 cells, allowing us to evaluate both their mean effect and the difference between
cell types (Siraj et al., 2026). Across 128 regulatory elements, AlphaGenome predicts the mean effect
with a correlation of 0.532, compared with 0.398 for the difference between them (Figure 3A/B). It
uses its existing prediction tracks without fitting to Siraj measurements. Linear readouts fitted to
NTv3 650M post reach 0.492 and 0.256, respectively. Both models predict the mean more accurately
than the difference, with a larger gap for NTv3.

Expression profiles distinguish tissues. We also evaluate how well models distinguish expression
profiles across tissues, using 5,782 held-out genes measured in 51 GTEx tissues (GTEx Consortium,
2020). For each gene, we pair each tissue with the tissue where measured expression differs most.
We then measure how well a model’s difference in expression prediction aligns with the observed
difference. Using native outputs, AlphaGenome reaches a correlation of 0.775, compared with
0.594 for NTv3 650M post (Figure 12A). Because the comparison tissue is chosen using measured
expression, this effectively tests model predictions for observed tissue contrasts.
A second test asks whether a predicted expression profile across genes identifies the correct tissue
among the 51 candidates. Using native outputs, AlphaGenome achieves 68.6% accuracy, compared
with 45.1% for NTv3 650M post and merely 7.8% for the k-mer ridge (Figure 12B). Random chance
has 2.0% accuracy, while the experimental reference identifies tissues with 99.0% accuracy. This
reference uses expression profiles from independent donor halves, averaging both directions. The
models generally allow for accurate tissue classification, but a sizeable performance gap remains.
The evaluations hold out genes within each tissue (see also Appendix D.1).


6   G ENETIC INTERACTIONS : PREDICTING DEPARTURES FROM ADDITIVITY

A mutation’s effect depends on its genetic background. Rather than the cell level context of
the previous section, different genetic backgrounds may also alter the effect of mutations. The
Siraj data allows for isolating this interaction, since it contains the same edit on two paired allelic
backgrounds. Across 68,026 paired mutations in 114 regulatory elements, AlphaGenome reaches
interaction correlation 0.260, compared with 0.187 for NTv3 650M post’s fitted readout and 0.181
for Enformer (Avsec et al., 2021) (Figure 4A). Predictions are most accurate for mutations within 10
bp of one another and become less accurate with increasing separation (Figure 4C).


Combined-effect accuracy can conceal weak interaction prediction. COMPASS tests non-
additive interactions on splicing by measuring single and double mutations relative to the same
reference. On its 110-gene panel, NTv3 650M post reaches correlation 0.409 for single-mutation
effects and 0.500 for double-mutation effects, but only 0.046 for the interaction remaining after
subtracting the two single effects (Figure 4B). Native splice predictors show the same pattern, and
replicate correlation for the interaction measurements is 0.171.
To assess how much interaction prediction contributes to overall accuracy, we remove the predicted
interaction and retain only the additive effects. NTv3’s double-mutation correlation changes only
from 0.500 to 0.493. Similarly, removing AlphaGenome’s predicted background dependence on Siraj
changes overall correlation from 0.528 to 0.521, a small but statistically supported decrease of 0.0070
(95% interval: 0.0014–0.0120; Appendix E.2). This indicates that a model can predict combined
effects accurately while recovering little of the interaction between mutations.

7    G ENOMIC DESIGN : SELECTING SEQUENCES FOR A DESIRED RESPONSE

We finally ask whether model predictions help choose DNA with a desired response. These tasks
evaluate the measured outcomes of model-selected candidates, progressing from matching a sin-
gle response to controlling expression across multiple cell types. All choices are made within
experimentally assayed libraries, with candidate measurements withheld from selection.


Matching a target response. Can a model find an alternative mutation that reproduces the effect of
a reference edit? In the Siraj assay, each element contains a natural variant and a measured change in
activity, which we define as the target response. Excluding that position leaves 578–597 candidate
substitutions per element. A model predicts an effect for every candidate and the reference edit,
then ranks candidates by how closely their predicted effects match its own reference prediction.
AlphaGenome scores effects as changes in its cell-matched accessibility outputs; NTv3 uses linear
heads evaluated on held-out elements. All candidates’ measured effects are withheld until scoring.
For evaluation, we rank edits by how closely their measured effects match the target. Across 331
targets in 110 elements, AlphaGenome’s first choice has an average percentile rank of 24.3, compared
with 30.0 for NTv3 650M post and 50 for uniform selection (Figure 5A; lower is better). The top ten
choices include at least one edit that matches the reference response within assay uncertainty for 62%
of targets with AlphaGenome and 59% with NTv3, compared with 38% under uniform selection
(Figure 5B). Model rankings can therefore help find matching edits with fewer tests.


Changing expression selectively. We also test a more challenging task, choosing edits which
increase expression in one cell type while minimally changing expression in another. We evaluate
167,958 substitutions across 280 parental backgrounds at 127 Siraj loci, with HepG2 and K562
alternately serving as the target cell. Each model chooses one substitution or no edit by maximizing
predicted utility Ucell = t − λ|o|, where t and o are expression changes relative to the unmutated
parent in the target and other cell. Six fixed penalties describe the tradeoff between the two responses
(Figure 5C). Complete loci were held out of readout fitting and native-output calibration.

8    C ONCLUSIONS

Genomic models capture functional information that transfers across datasets and genes. This genomic
understanding helps them select DNA with a desired target response. Functional training largely
expands these capabilities: predictions from embedding-fitted linear heads also match or exceed
native outputs, suggesting that its benefits extend beyond the prediction heads and affect the models
more deeply. At the same time, accurate aggregate predictions can conceal weak understanding
of cell types and genetic interactions, which on the other hand are highly important for practical
downstream use. BEND 2.0 focuses on the capabilities that matter for genomic design, hoping to
support the development of models that make this search more effective.

Tasks with less consistent experimental results are often the more difficult tasks for the models,
meaning performance should be interpreted in light of the experiment’s reliability. The chosen
experimental references are somewhat naive (for example, transferring mutation effects across cell
types), yet they still beat current models. The linear heads may also depend on the embedding sizes of
the models, and models with different input context lengths may have an effect on task performance.
Despite these limits, BEND 2.0 shows where genomic models may help narrow hundreds of candidate
mutations to a testable few, and where further progress is needed.

A.1    P REDICTIONS AND READOUTS

Native outputs and fitted linear heads. We evaluate models using native outputs and embedding-
fitted linear heads. Native outputs are produced by the models’ existing output heads. Depending
on the model, these include self-supervised sequence logits, supervised predictions of functional
measurements or genomic annotations. Embedding-fitted linear heads are trained on sequence

Experimental references. For experimental references, we use one set of measurements to make
predictions or choose sequences, then evaluate these predictions or choices using another set. De-
pending on the setting, the references can be somewhat naive (for example, transferring effects across
cell types) or stronger (predicting the same response using repeat measurements).
The split is dependent on the experimental data:

       • For OpenSplice, we compare the average of two variant PSI repeats with the third, averaging
         all three rotations. Effects share the reported wild-type estimate.
       • For Siraj, the background-interaction reference compares measurements between HepG2
         and K562. Other references compare effects calculated separately from repeats 1–2 and 3–5.
         We evaluate model predictions against effects estimated from all repeats. For cell-selective
         design, we instead evaluate both model choices and experimental references using effects
         relative to the unmutated parent.
       • For GTEx, tissue identification averages both donor-half directions (99.0%), and its uncer-
         tainty was not re-estimated.
       • For Atlas, each of the two RNA measurements is evaluated against the other.

For the Siraj data, the references for cell effects use 127 of the 128 Siraj elements, as one lacks the
required count data. Ranking and target-response matching use all 331 targets in 110 elements. We
select on one repeat group, test on the other, then swap the groups and average the results.

B.1     H OW POST- TRAINING CHANGES ACCESS TO THE SEQUENCE PRIOR

Additional masking improves base prediction in post-trained NTv3 despite — somewhat counterintu-
itively — removing more of the surrounding DNA context (Figure 1B). We investigate this effect and
test whether the improved performance can be reproduced while only masking the target base.

Mask placement. We test both NTv3 sizes before and after post-training on the 512 benchmark
windows. Each query masks the target base and alters 144 other positions, using two random draws.
Masks are spread across the window, placed within 128 bases of the target, or kept more than 256
bases away. For distant masks, we compare scattered and compact patterns with the same number of
masks on each side and matched average distances. We also replace the same distant bases with other
nucleotides instead of MASK tokens.
For 650M post, distant scattered masks give lower loss than compact masks (1.794 versus 2.167 bits;
paired difference 0.373, 95% interval 0.283–0.467). Mask count and average distance therefore do
not explain the difference. Base replacement does not reproduce the benefit. The 100M post model
behaves similarly, while pre-trained models change much less (Figure 8A–B).

Replacing transformer representations. NTv3 predicts bases using both its final transformer
representation and convolutional features that bypass the transformer (skip features) (Boshar et al.,
2025). With only the target base masked, we replace the transformer representation with one obtained
from the same DNA with extra masks or base replacements. We keep the skip features fixed, then
test the reverse: replacing only the skip features.
For 650M post, uniform masking lowers loss by 0.821 bits. Replacing only the transformer represen-
tation reproduces 0.812 bits of this improvement. Replacing only the skip features changes loss by
+0.014 bits, with an interval that includes zero. Distant masks and the 100M post model show the
same pattern (Figure 8C).

Using unrelated DNA. We then test whether the masking benefit depends on the sequence that was
masked. Using 256 pairs of unrelated sequences with similar GC content, we compare transformer
representations from the unrelated DNA with and without extra distant masks. The sequence being
predicted and its skip features stay fixed.

Predicting the carried allele. We score all four bases in each carrier’s own surrounding DNA,

F     G ENOMIC DESIGN : TARGET MATCHING AND SELECTIVE EXPRESSION

F.1    M ATCHING A TARGET RESPONSE

In the Siraj assay, we start from a regulatory element’s reference sequence. The reference edit
δ0 introduces an alternative allele. We ask whether a single-base edit at any other position in the
reference sequence can produce a similar change in RNA activity. We also test the reverse direction.
Notably, Siraj has experimentally measured the entire design space, thus enabling this task.
We retain 331 targets across 110 elements for which the measured reference-edit effect is at least
twice its standard error in magnitude. Each model ranks candidate edits by how closely their predicted
effects match its own prediction for δ0 .
For the first selected edit, we report the percentile 100(r − 1)/(N − 1), where r ranks its measured
distance from the target among N eligible edits. Lower percentiles are hence closer matches.

Success within a testing budget. For a budget of B tests, TopBm contains the first B edits selected
by model m. A target is matched if at least one selected edit differs from the measured reference
effect by no more than one combined standard error:

                                        |∆(η | x, c) − ∆(δ0 | x, c)|
                      SB = 1     min        p                         ≤1 .                     (15)
                               η∈TopBm        SE(η)2 + SE(δ0 )2

Here, ∆ is the measured change in RNA activity and SE its standard error. Both percentile ranks and
success rates are averaged over targets within each element, then equally across elements.

Predicting effects. We compare AlphaGenome and Enformer native accessibility outputs, NTv3

across backgrounds and target cells within each locus, then equally across loci. Choosing no edit

The control makes an edit for exactly the same parent and target-cell combinations as the selective
method. It mixes six target-only selection rules covering weaker and stronger predicted effects,
matching the selective method’s average predicted target effect within each combination. Other-cell
predictions do not rank the control’s candidates, and measured responses are not used to fit the
mixture. The matching therefore applies to predictions; measured RNA effects can still differ.
We use 50,000 bootstrap samples of whole loci, keeping fitted models, calibration factors and selected

Bonferroni correction.

and both NTv3 readouts also improve over their matched controls. Enformer’s gain over its control is
uncertain: 0.055 (adjusted 95% interval −0.011–0.120).

using repeats 3–5, then reverse the groups and average the results. Both directions use the same
candidates, penalties, no-edit option and control procedure.
The reference reaches mean utility 0.962 (95% locus-bootstrap interval 0.829–1.092), with gains of
0.371 over target-only selection and 0.292 over the matched control. On average, edits chosen using
one group of measurements remain selective in the other.
